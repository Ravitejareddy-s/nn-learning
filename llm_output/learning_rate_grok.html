Step size: how big a hop, how do you know you didn't overshoot, and when do you stop?

You asked the three questions that are the whole topic of learning-rate tuning. Short answer first, then the pictures — all drawn from real gradient descent on a toy bowl-shaped loss.

the whole thing in one line

You don't know the right step size up front — you probe for it and watch the loss curve. Too small → loss crawls. Too big → loss goes up and blows up. You pick the biggest step that still goes down smoothly, and you stop when the curve flattens.

1. The picture behind all of it: loss is a landscape

Your loss is a number that depends on the weights. Plot it and you get a landscape. For the bigram net it's a smooth bowl (one lowest point). The gradient at your spot points uphill; you take a step downhill (against it). The learning rate is how long that step is. That's literally all it is.

The gradient only tells you the direction (which way is down) and roughly how steep — it does not tell you how far to walk. Picking the distance is on you. That distance is the learning rate.

2. Four step sizes on the exact same bowl

Same start (w = 9), same bowl, only the step size changes. Watch the ball. The update here is w ← w - lr·(2w), i.e. w gets multiplied by r = 1 - 2·lr every step — that one number decides the fate:

Orange: step so tiny it inches down — safe but wastes runs (this was your lr=0.1). Green: lands near the bottom in a few hops. Blue: step overshoots the bottom and lands on the other wall, but a bit lower each time, so it still spirals in. Red: the step overshoots so hard it lands higher than it started — every hop is worse, the loss explodes to infinity / NaN. That is “blowing up.”

How you know you overshot: the loss increased after a step. A step can overshoot the bottom (land on the far wall) and still be fine as long as it lands lower — that's the blue case. It's only fatal when each hop lands higher than the last (red). So the test isn't “did I cross the bottom?” — it's “is the loss still going down?”

3. The one thing you actually watch: the loss curve

You never see the landscape directly (it lives in 729 dimensions for your net). What you do see is loss vs iteration, and its shape diagnoses the step size for you:

4. So how do you PICK the number? Sweep it.

The honest professional answer: you don't compute it, you search for it. Try a range of learning rates, run each for a handful of steps, and plot the resulting loss against the lr you used. This is Karpathy's learning-rate finder (you'll build exactly this in lecture 3):

Loss is high for tiny lr (too slow to get anywhere), dips into a sweet-spot valley, then shoots up off a cliff once the step is too big. You pick the lr at the edge of the cliff — the largest one that's still going down. Big enough to be fast, not so big it diverges.

5. “How many runs without blowing up?”

Two separate worries hide in that question:

  • Blowing up is not caused by too many iterations — it's caused by too big a step. With a good lr you can run as long as you like and it never explodes; it just flattens.
  • How many iterations = run until the loss curve plateaus. When each new run barely moves the number, more runs won't help — you've hit the floor for this lr. That's your stop signal, not a fixed count.

And the trick that gets you both fast and precise: learning-rate decay — start with a big step to cover ground, then shrink it to settle in cleanly (like taking big strides across a field, then tiny careful steps once you're right over the drain):

Blue (constant big lr) keeps bouncing around the bottom forever. Green shrinks its step partway through and drops straight onto the minimum. Karpathy does exactly this in lecture 3 (10x smaller lr near the end).

6. Back to your bigram net: why lr=50 works there but 1.0 blows up here

the mind-bender

In this toy bowl, lr just past 1.0 already explodes. In your bigram net, lr=50 is perfect. How? Because the gradient scale is totally different. Here the gradient is 2w (big). In your net the loss is an average over 228k examples of softmax probabilities, so W.grad is tiny — a step of lr · grad needs a big lr just to move an appreciable amount. This is the punchline: there is no universal “good” learning rate. The right value depends on how big the gradients happen to be for your loss, which is exactly why you probe instead of memorizing a number. Your lr=0.1 wasn't “wrong” — it was just far too small for this problem's gradient scale, so it sat in the orange “crawls” regime.

keep this

Step size is a search, not a formula. Watch loss vs iteration: crawling → bigger lr; flattening → stop; rising/NaN → overshoot, cut lr. Pick the biggest lr that still descends (edge of the cliff in the sweep), optionally decay it to land cleanly. Blowing up comes from too-big steps, not too many runs. And because the “right” lr rides on your loss's gradient scale, 0.1 vs 50 can both be correct — for different problems.
