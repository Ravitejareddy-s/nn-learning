Standard deviation & (hpreact − mean) / std: what BatchNorm is doing at 45:00

You already own the mean. This page builds std from nothing, explains why Karpathy takes it down the batch (dim=0), and shows what the whole line does — each idea with a picture, exact numbers computed in Python.

the whole thing in one breath

Standard deviation = the typical distance of the points from their average. Karpathy computes mean & std per neuron (one number per column, over the 32 examples) because a neuron is a feature, and its health is a property measured across many examples. Then (hpreact − mean)/std rewrites every firing value as “how many typical-distances am I from my neuron's average” — which forces each neuron to mean 0, std 1, the shape tanh likes best.

0. Mean, just to set the stage

The mean is the balance point of the values. If the dots were weights on a ruler, the mean is where you'd put the fulcrum so it doesn't tip. Toy neuron firing on 5 examples: 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 → mean 50.

1. Standard deviation: “how spread out”, built step by step

Mean tells you the center. It says nothing about whether the points hug that center or fly all over. 30,40,50,60,70 and 48,49,50,51,52 have the same mean but feel completely different. Std is the number that captures that difference. Build it in two moves.

Move 1 — measure each point's distance from the mean

Natural first idea: just average these distances. It fails — the points below the mean give negative distances, the ones above give positive, and they cancel to exactly 0 (they must, that's what “balance point” means). So raw distances can't measure spread.

Move 2 — square, average, square-root

Fix the cancelling by squaring each distance (a square is always positive, and it punishes far-away points harder). Average the squares — that average is the variance (here 200). Variance is now in “squared units,” so finally take the square root to get back to the original scale: that's the standard deviation = 14.14.

Read std in plain words: “the points of this neuron sit, typically about 14 away from their average of 50.” Small std = tight cluster; big std = scattered. That's the entire idea.

where it comes from

Karl Pearson coined the very name “standard deviation” in 1893 — before that people used clunkier terms like “mean error.” And the choice to square (instead of taking absolute values) traces to Gauss: squared error is what makes the math close cleanly and is exactly what produces the bell curve below. Squaring wasn't arbitrary; it's the choice that made the whole theory of the normal distribution work.

Why std is the natural ruler: the bell curve

For a bell-shaped (gaussian) spread, std has a crisp meaning: about 68% of the mass lands within 1 std of the mean, about 95% within 2 std. So “2 std away” means the same level of rare no matter the raw scale. “Unit gaussian” = mean 0, std 1 — that phrase Karpathy keeps saying is just this bell, recentred and rescaled.

2. Why the mean/std is taken dim=0 (down the batch)

Your instinct: “we usually summarize a record.” Here's the flip that fixes it — in almost every dataset you actually summarize a feature down the rows, not a record across its features. To normalize people's heights you take the mean/std of the height column over all people; you'd never average one person's height, weight and age together. A neuron is that feature/column. So going down the column (dim=0) is the normal move, and going across a row would be the strange one.

hpreact.mean(0, keepdim=True) collapses the row dimension: for each of the 200 neurons it averages that neuron over all 32 examples, giving a 1×200 row of per-neuron means (and likewise for std). keepdim=True keeps the shape 1×200 so it broadcasts cleanly back over the 32 rows when we subtract.

the subtle catch (bonus)

Because each neuron's mean/std is computed from the batch, one example's normalized value now depends on which other examples happen to share its batch. That coupling is unusual (and is why BatchNorm needs a separate running mean/std at test time, which Karpathy handles right after this). It also acts as a mild regularizer. File it away — it's the root of the train/test wrinkle coming up.

3. What (hpreact − mean) / std actually gives

Two moves on each neuron's column, and both are things you now understand:

  • − mean: slide the values so their center sits at 0. Now each number reads as “how far above/below this neuron's average.”
  • ÷ std: rescale so the typical distance becomes exactly 1. Now each number reads as “how many typical distances (std units) from average” — this is called a z-score.

The output column always comes out at mean 0, std 1 — a unit gaussian — no matter how big or shifted the raw values were. It's the same idea as grading on a curve: a raw mark of 85 tells you little, but “you were 1.4 std above the class average” places you exactly. z-scores are marks re-expressed in surprise units.

Why force that shape at all? Look at tanh

This closes the loop with the first half of the lecture. If hpreact is spread too wide, most values land in tanh's flat tails (output stuck near ±1, slope ~0, no gradient flows — the “saturated tanh” problem). Squeezing every neuron to unit gaussian drops the values into tanh's active middle band, where the curve actually bends and gradients survive.

keep this

std = typical distance from the mean (square the distances so they don't cancel, average = variance, sqrt back = std). dim=0 = per-neuron, because a neuron is a feature and features are summarized down the rows. (x−mean)/std = z-score, which re-expresses each value in std-units and lands every neuron at mean 0, std 1 — the shape that keeps tanh awake. The learnable bngain/bnbias Karpathy adds right after just let the network scale/shift away from that default if it wants to.
