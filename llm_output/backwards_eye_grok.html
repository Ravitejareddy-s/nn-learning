Your eye is wired backwards

The light sensors face the wrong way, with the wiring and blood vessels draped in front of them. Evolution never fixed it — it just patched around it. That's Esteban Real's exact point about evolutionary search, told in biology.

1. What "backwards" means

Light enters from the left. In a sensible design it would hit the sensors first. Instead it must pass through nerve wiring and a mesh of blood vessels before reaching the photoreceptors pinned at the very back, facing away from the light.

light nerve fibers (wiring) blood vessels photoreceptors (the actual sensors) pigment layer

Why this happened: the vertebrate retina grows as an outpouching of the brain during development, and that fold flips the layer order. It's a frozen accident baked into how the eye is built, not a physics requirement.

2. The worst side-effect: a literal hole in your vision

Because the wiring sits on the front, all those nerve fibers have to punch a hole straight through the retina to escape to the brain as the optic nerve. At that hole there are no photoreceptors — so each eye has a real blind spot. You don't notice it, which is the whole point of the next section.

3. How evolution "solved" it — by patching, never redesigning

Evolution can't flip the retina around, because every half-flipped intermediate would be a worse eye, and natural selection can't walk through a valley of blindness to reach a better peak on the other side. So instead it bolted on a stack of clever workarounds:

The problemEvolution's patch
The blind-spot holeYour brain fills it in with surrounding color, your two eyes cover each other's holes, and constant tiny eye-movements smear it away. You never see it.
Wiring blurs the sharpest visionAt the fovea (center of gaze) the overlying layers are shoved aside into a pit, so light hits those cones almost directly.
Light scatters in the messy front layersMüller cells act like fibre-optic light guides, funnelling light through the clutter down to the sensors.
Blood vessels cast shadowsThe vessels are fixed on the retina, so your brain learns their shadow and permanently subtracts it.

None of these fix the root cause. They're all downstream duct-tape — exactly the flavour of solution evolutionary search produces.

4. Proof it wasn't necessary: the octopus got it right

The octopus eye evolved completely separately and ended up "correct": sensors face the light, wiring tucked behind, no blind spot. Same problem, better solution — which proves the vertebrate mess is just a historical accident, not a law of optics.

5. Why this is the whole point of the talk

Esteban's silicon story: "we'd accidentally leave a bug in the code, and evolution would find a way to work around it — so well we didn't even notice the bug."

The eye is the same story in biology: a "bug" (backwards wiring) got frozen in early, and evolution built a pile of workarounds — brain fill-in, the fovea pit, Müller fibre-optics — instead of fixing the root cause.

Same principle, and it's exactly why the conv → ReLU → ReLU → ReLU that his search produced doesn't bother him: evolution optimizes for "does it work," not "is it clean." It lands on local optima full of harmless quirks and duct-tape, never the elegant redesign a human engineer would draw. Powerful and weird, in silicon and in your own eyeball.
