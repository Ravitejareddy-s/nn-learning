exp and log: why changing the numbers doesn't change the answer

Your exact worry, first: “exp and log rescale the value of x. If the numbers change, how can the result be trusted?” This page answers that, starting from the shape of each function and ending at why they fit softmax + loss perfectly.

The whole resolution in one line

Both functions are strictly increasing relabelings of the number line. A relabeling that never reorders anything keeps the two things we actually use — which value is bigger, and where the best answer sits — and only changes raw magnitudes, which we never use. Change a ruler's markings however you like; the tallest kid is still the tallest.

Hold that sentence. Everything below is just making it concrete and then cashing it in twice: once for exp (building probabilities) and once for log (measuring them).

1. The shape of e^x

Feed e^x any real number and read off four properties straight from the curve:

  • Always positive. The whole real line (negatives included) maps into (0, infinity). It never touches zero, never goes negative.
  • Always rising. Bigger x gives bigger output, no exceptions — that's the “never reorders” property.
  • Equal steps in x give a constant ratio, not a constant gap. Each +1 in x multiplies the output by e (~2.72). So the gaps grow, but the multiply factor is fixed. This is the “same distance” you were sensing — it's a constant ratio, not a constant gap.
  • Its slope equals its own height. That's why it steepens forever, and why it's a curve rather than a line.
where e comes from

Jacob Bernoulli hit e in the 1680s studying compound interest: interest on your interest on your interest is repeated multiplication, and compounding it continuously converges to e ~ 2.71828. That's the same “each step multiplies” behaviour you see in the curve.

2. The shape of log(x)

log is e^x seen in a mirror. It takes a positive number and asks “e to what power gives this?” Read its properties:

  • Input positive, output any real. It maps (0, infinity) onto the whole number line — exactly the reverse of exp.
  • log(1) = 0, and it dives toward minus-infinity as x approaches 0.
  • Always rising — again, never reorders.
  • It turns multiply into add. log(a x b) = log a + log b. Each doubling of x adds a fixed 0.69; each x10 adds a fixed 2.30.
where log comes from

John Napier published logarithms in 1614 for one reason: to turn brutal hand-multiplication of huge astronomical numbers into simple addition (via printed log tables and later the slide rule). “Multiply becomes add” wasn't a side effect — it was the entire point. Keep that; it's exactly why log shows up in the loss.

3. They are inverses — a lossless relabel

Because log(e^x) = x and e^(log y) = y, the two curves are reflections across the line y = x. That's the deep reason your worry dissolves: going through exp and coming back through log lands you exactly where you started. No information is lost. It's a faithful relabel of the axis, like Celsius ↔ Fahrenheit, not a distortion of the facts.

4. The picture that answers your question

Here is the crux. Imagine sliding one weight w and plotting how good the model is (top). It rises to a best value, then falls. Now take the log of that height (bottom). The curve looks totally different — but the peak sits at the exact same w.

Stretching the height axis cannot move the summit. Since training only ever asks “which w is best?”, and a monotonic relabel never moves the best point, optimizing the log gives the identical answer as optimizing the original — while being far easier to compute with. The scale changed; the answer did not.

5. Cashing in exp: building probabilities (softmax)

The net spits out logits — arbitrary scores, some negative. We need a probability distribution: all positive, summing to 1. Watch what each requirement demands, and notice exp is the tool that satisfies both at once:

  • Must be positive → exp maps every score (even negative) to a positive number.
  • Must keep the ranking the net learned → exp is monotonic, so the biggest logit stays the biggest probability. It doesn't invent an order; it preserves the one the net chose.
  • Must sum to 1 → divide by the total.

Tallest bar stays A the whole way through: exp never reorders. The negative logits (C, D) become small but strictly positive counts -- so no probability is ever 0, and no log ever blows up.

What exp decides is only how much more likely a higher score is: a logit one unit bigger becomes e× more count. That's the single modelling choice — convert score gaps into probability ratios — and it's the natural one precisely because exp turns additive scores into multiplicative weights.

6. Cashing in log: measuring them (the loss)

To score the model on a name, multiply the probabilities it gave each correct next letter. That product is the likelihood. But a product of many numbers below 1 collapses toward zero almost instantly (left) — ugly to read and prone to underflow. Take logs and the product becomes a tidy running sum (right, Napier's trick):

Two payoffs, both already proven above: the sum is numerically sane, and — because log is monotonic (§4) — the weights that maximize the product are the same weights that maximize the sum of logs. We then flip the sign so that low = good (a loss) and average over the dataset. That is the average negative log-likelihood.

Why log also gives a clean notion of “surprise”

-log(p) is how surprised the model is by an outcome. Because log turns multiply into add, every time an event gets 10× rarer, the surprise goes up by the same fixed amount (2.30). Surprise stacks by addition — which is exactly what a loss you sum over a dataset needs.

7. Why they're a matched pair here

exp turns scores into positive, correctly-ranked weights you can normalize into probabilities. log turns those probabilities back into an additive, well-behaved score to optimize. They're inverses, so the round trip is consistent — and every step only ever leans on ordering and positivity, never on the raw magnitudes that changed.

bonus origin

“Softmax” is lifted from physics: the Boltzmann distribution e^(-E/kT) gives the probability a system sits in a state of energy E. Your logits play the role of (negative) energies. Same exp-then-normalize shape, invented for a completely different reason a century earlier.

keep this

exp and log rescale the numbers but never reorder them and never move the best point. Softmax needs only positivity + ordering; the loss needs only the location of the best weights. All of that survives the rescale — so changing the scale costs nothing, and buys positivity (exp) and add-instead-of-multiply (log).
